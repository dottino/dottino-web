How to help your child prepare for ABRSM Grade 4 aural

Aural is the part of the exam most parents feel they can’t help with. You can. You don’t need to read music; you need to know what to listen for, and how to ask.

By Tim · piano teacher for 15+ years, Royal College of Music, PhD in music education · Updated 30 October 2026

Why children guess in aural

When I ask a child what they heard, the most common answer is a guess. Not because they can’t hear it, but because nobody has told them what to listen for. “Is it major or minor?” is a hard question. “Where does the music end, bright or dark?” is an easy one. Most of aural practice is turning big questions into small ones.

The other reason is speed. Children answer as soon as the music starts, before they have heard enough. A useful habit, at home and in the exam room, is three words: pause, listen, check.

What the Grade 4 aural tests ask

At Grade 4 there are three tests: A, B and C, and C has two parts. Here is what each one asks, in plain English, and a short game you can play at home for each.

ASinging or playing back a melody

The examiner plays a short melody twice. Your child sings it back (or plays it, if they prefer) from memory, keeping in time.

At home: sing a short tune from a song they know and ask them to echo it back. Then make it one note longer. If they get lost, ask: “Did the tune go up or down at the end?” before trying again.

BSinging five notes from music

Your child sees five notes written on a stave and sings them. They hear the key chord and the first note first, and can take their time.

At home: this is the test children fear most, usually because they have never sung in front of anyone. Sing together first, quietly, then let them lead. Humming the notes is a good first step.

C(i)Talking about a short piece

The examiner plays a short piece and asks about one feature, such as dynamics (loud and soft), articulation (smooth or detached), tempo, or whether it is major or minor. Then a second question about the character of the music, and what in the music creates it.

At home: put on any piece of music for 20 seconds and ask one question only: “Did it get louder, quieter, or stay the same?” Next time, a different question. A one-word answer is fine; then ask “What made you think that?”

C(ii)Clapping a rhythm and naming the time

The examiner plays part of the same piece again. Your child claps the rhythm back, then says whether it is in 2, 3 or 4 time.

At home: tap the strong beats of a song on your knees together and count out loud between them. Marches and most pop songs feel like 2 or 4; waltzes feel like 3.

This is a summary in everyday language. Exam details can change, so check the current ABRSM syllabus and ask your child’s teacher before the exam.

A four-week plan for home

Five to ten minutes on most days does more than one long session a week. Here is a simple shape, one skill a week, each building on the last.

WeekFocusA daily 5-minute game
1Up or down, same or differentPlay or sing two notes: higher or lower? Then two short tunes: same or different?
2Echo a melody (Test A)Echo short tunes, adding one note each day.
3Beats and rhythm (Test C(ii))Tap the strong beat to songs. Is it 2, 3 or 4? Clap back short rhythms.
4Describing music (Test C(i))One question per piece: loud or soft? smooth or detached? bright or dark? Then: what made you think that?

Leave singing from notes (Test B) to the teacher at first, then practise it together once your child feels safe singing at home.

What to say when they get it wrong

How you react to a wrong answer matters more than the answer. A child who is afraid of being wrong will guess faster, not listen more.

Instead of “No, it’s minor.”

Try “Let’s listen to the very end again. Does it finish bright or dark?”

Replay first, answer second. A wrong answer followed by a second listen is exactly how the skill grows.

Questions parents ask

Can I help if I don’t read music?

Yes. Most aural practice is listening and talking. You don’t need to know the answer to ask a good question; asking “what made you think that?” is enough.

How long should we practise each day?

Five to ten minutes on most days. Short and often beats long and rare.

My child just guesses. What should I do?

Ask one small, specific question at a time, and replay the music before giving any answer. Guessing usually means nobody has said what to listen for yet.

Do we need a piano at home?

It helps for playing back melodies but isn’t essential. Recordings, singing and clapping cover most of it.
